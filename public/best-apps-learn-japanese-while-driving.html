The best apps for learning Japanese while driving (2026).

Seven apps genuinely work behind the wheel — and they're good at different things. Quick version: Pimsleur for a guided conversational course, Kotoba (free) or Danki ($9.99 once) for vocabulary with real feedback, JapanesePod101 for a listening library, Language Transfer for free grammar-by-ear. Anki and Duolingo don't make the list — they need your eyes.

Disclosure: this list is published by the developer of Kotoba, one of the apps below. To keep it useful we've included every serious competitor, said plainly what each does better, and ranked by use case instead of putting ourselves first. Corrections welcome via GitHub.

1 · Pimsleur — best guided audio course

Pick it for: scripted conversational lessons with professional pacing · ~$20/month

The category veteran, and still the best pure course for the car. Thirty-minute lessons walk you through dialogues with call-and-response prompts and graduated review, all listenable end-to-end with a driving-mode UI. Its limits: it can't hear you (the answer pause is an honor system), lessons are identical for everyone, and vocabulary coverage per hour is thin. If you want structure and polish and don't mind the subscription, start here — our deeper look: a Pimsleur alternative that actually hears you.

2 · Kotoba — best for vocabulary, best free option

Pick it for: JLPT vocabulary with speech-recognition grading · Free

Our app, so apply salt — but the design brief was exactly this list's premise. Kotoba runs a complete spaced-repetition session by voice: it teaches each word with a slow spoken breakdown and a native example sentence, quizzes you days later, hears and grades your spoken answer, and schedules every word with FSRS. "Repeat", "skip", and "pause" are voice commands; one tap on Start is the only touch. Covers JLPT N5–N3 (~3,000 words, 40,000+ native audio clips), runs in the browser with no app store, works offline once cached. Limits: Japanese only, vocabulary-first (no grammar lectures), and no custom deck import yet.

3 · Danki — best one-time purchase (iOS)

Pick it for: a polished native iOS drive mode without a subscription · $9.99 once

A newer Japanese-focused app with a purpose-built "Audio Drive Mode": vocabulary and sentences through your car speakers, configurable recall pauses, voice commands ("good" / "again"), FSRS scheduling, and native audio. It doesn't verify your answer with speech recognition — grading is by voice command — but it's a well-executed take on the same idea, and the one-time price is refreshing. iOS only.

4 · JapanesePod101 — best listening library

Pick it for: a huge podcast-style lesson catalog · Freemium

Thousands of host-led audio lessons from absolute-beginner to advanced, with autoplay queues that run hands-free. It's passive — nothing checks you — but as listening-comprehension volume it's unmatched, and the free tier plus YouTube channel are real. Works well alongside an interactive trainer: comprehension from JPod, recall from your SRS.

5 · Language Transfer / Michel Thomas — best grammar by ear

Pick it for: understanding how Japanese works, purely through audio · Free / ~$100 one-time

Both teach grammar intuition through guided audio reasoning — "how would you say…?" — and both are excellent commute material for the structure of the language. Language Transfer's Complete Japanese is free (donation-supported); Michel Thomas is the polished paid original. Neither hears you, and neither builds vocabulary volume; they're the grammar hour, not the vocab hour.

6 · Audio Flash — best if your decks live in Anki

Pick it for: listening to existing Anki decks with timed recall pauses · Freemium

General-purpose audio flashcards: import an Anki deck, and it reads prompt → pause → answer with TTS, offline, with shuffle/SRS-ish modes. Grading is the honor system — nothing registers whether you recalled the word, so scheduling can't adapt — but if your custom deck is the asset you care about, this is the most direct way to get it into the car. Context: why Anki itself can't do this.

7 · JLPT Speak — speaking-graded JLPT drills

Pick it for: typed-Anki-style JLPT review where you speak instead of type · Paid app

Flashcard-style JLPT N5–N3 review where answers are spoken and checked, with spaced repetition. Conceptually the closest cousin to Kotoba on this list; the experience is built around the phone screen more than a fully eyes-free session, which is why it ranks here on a driving list specifically.

Why Anki and Duolingo aren't on the list

Not because they're bad — because they're screen apps. Duolingo's exercises are taps and reading. Anki has no audio-only or background mode (a feature request open for years), and the auto-advance workaround feeds its scheduler fake grades. Keep both for the desk; details in Anki while driving and audio spaced repetition, explained.

Frequently asked questions

Which one should I actually start with?

If you're brand new and want to be led: Pimsleur (or Language Transfer, free). If your bottleneck is vocabulary — it usually is — start a free Kotoba session on tomorrow's commute and keep whatever passive audio you already enjoy for variety.

Can I combine them?

The combinations that work: an interactive SRS (Kotoba/Danki) for vocab + JapanesePod101 or Language Transfer for comprehension and grammar. Alternate days or split the drive.

How is this list maintained?

We re-check pricing and features every month or two and note the updated date at the top. Something wrong or missing? Open an issue on GitHub — genuinely welcome, including for competitors.
